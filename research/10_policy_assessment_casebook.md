## How to use this casebook

Each case answers a **precise counterfactual policy question** by applying already verified PolisLab evidence: what the law establishes, what empirics support, which mechanisms are plausible, what could reverse the prediction, and what cannot presently be concluded.

| Label | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| **Layer A** | Law in force |
| **Layer B** | Administrative practice / observed outcomes |
| **Layer C** | Announced reforms not treated as current entitlements |
| **Layer D** | Hypothetical PolisLab instruments |

**Atlas case membership.** The CSV field `casebook_cases` means **direct citation** of that claim ID in the named case body (or in this front-matter where a hard rule is stated to apply to all four cases). It does **not** mean every background source that might be relevant.

**Hard rules retained from Phases 0B–0E** (**ATLAS-ASM-002**; **ATLAS-CON-003** — all cases)

1. Access ≠ capacity ≠ viability ≠ survival ≠ durable constraint ≠ welfare (**O1–O6**).  
2. Absence of an ACCC land-banking finding is **not** proof that foreclosure is absent.  
3. US/UK magnitudes are **not** Australian parameters.  
4. Entry counts, planning approvals, and acquisition notifications do **not** imply welfare.  
5. Phase 0D.2 toy-game propositions are **not** validated analytical results (**ATLAS-HYP-002**).  
6. ALDI SA/WA 2016 chronology is an **O2 benchmark**, not a causal estimate (**ATLAS-HYP-004**).

**Mechanism shorthand:** **M1** site/planning/foreclosure · **M2** scale/logistics · **M3** demand/format · **M4** incumbent response · **M5** finance/sunk/exit · **M6** FDI mode/frictions.

## Case 1 — Undeveloped supermarket sites

### 1. Precise policy question

Relative to **current Australian law** (post-2026 merger notification, CCA potential, FIRB foreign vacant-land conditions where triggered), would either of the following **Layer D** instruments be expected — on existing evidence — to improve rival **market access (O1)** and subsequent **productive capacity (O2)** at undeveloped supermarket-capable sites:

- **R — Develop-or-release:** a duty on controllers of strategically important undeveloped supermarket sites to develop within a stated deadline or release control; or  
- **E — Exclusivity restriction:** a categorical limit or ban on grocery exclusivity / exclusionary covenants that prevent rival grocery use of a controlled site or centre?

The assessment **does not presume** that site release improves competition.

### 2. Legally accurate baseline

| Element | What current law/institutions do | Atlas IDs |
|---------|----------------------------------|-----------|
| Unused incumbent pipelines | ACCC inquiry documented undeveloped/unused pipelines intended for future supermarket use: ALDI **17**, Coles **59**, Woolworths **105** (Coles+Woolworths **164**) — **descriptive**, not a divestiture order | ATLAS-EMP-001 |
| Nationwide exclusionary land banking | Inquiry **did not** determine case-by-case land banking; outside scope — **absence of finding** | ATLAS-EMP-002 |
| Major-supermarket land/business acquisitions | Mandatory ACCC notification for Coles/Woolworths connected entities under Determination ss 1-7, 3-1–3-3 (size tests; carve-outs; sunset 30 Jun 2030) | ATLAS-LEG-006; ATLAS-LEG-007 |
| Foreign vacant commercial land | Generally $0 **notification** for foreign vacant commercial land; Guidance Note 4 separately describes **typical** productive-use / ~5-year construction **conditions** — foreign acquirers, not a domestic incumbent release statute | ATLAS-LEG-003 |
| Restrictive covenants / exclusivity | CCA can reach anti-competitive provisions; historical undertakings exist; **no verified comprehensive statutory grocery-exclusion ban** matching a blanket PolisLab rule | ATLAS-POL-002 |
| Numeric local site-share caps | **No verified statute**; 40% cap is Layer D only | ATLAS-POL-004 |

**Baseline framing:** Australia is **not** a site-control vacuum after 1 January 2026 (**ATLAS-ASM-001**). What remains additional is automatic release, numeric caps, and a blanket exclusivity ban beyond case-by-case CCA/merger tools (**ATLAS-POL-001**).

### 3. Counterfactual interventions (defined)

| Instrument | Definition used here | Explicitly **not** claimed |
|------------|----------------------|----------------------------|
| **R** | If a designated controller holds exclusive undeveloped control of a qualifying supermarket-capable site past a statutory milestone, it must **develop** (open or commence continuous development under defined criteria) or **release** control into an open assignment process | That released sites go to rivals; that deadlines are constitutionally settled; that R restates FIRB practice |
| **E** | Grocery exclusivity clauses / covenants that prevent rival supermarket use on the controlled parcel or designated centre are **void or prohibited**, subject to any transitional carve-outs | That E is costless for landlord development inducement; that E equals R |

Both are **Layer D**. Legal feasibility of compulsory release / property-rights characterisation remains **UNVERIFIED** specialist territory (Phase 0B gap).

### 4. Affected causal mechanisms

| Mechanism | Role |
|-----------|------|
| **M1a** physical/commercial scarcity | Unused sites may reflect genuine scarcity of *other* viable parcels |
| **M1b** planning/zoning | Holding may track permission delay, contamination, liquor, or centre design — not exclusion |
| **M1c** strategic foreclosure | Holding/options/covenants *may* raise rivals’ costs — **live hypothesis**, not proven general pattern (**ATLAS-HYP-001**) |
| **M4a** ordinary rivalry | Leasehold expansion can be competitive rivalry with exit-risk side effects — illustrated by the ACCC’s Kalgoorlie determination and its prospective theory of harm (**ATLAS-ADM-002**); distinct from M1c |
| **M4b** exclusionary response | Preemption via sites/covenants/acquisitions of nascent constraints |
| **M5a** irreversible investment / option value | Vacant control can be efficient waiting under uncertainty |

**Required separation:** scarcity ≠ planning ≠ foreclosure. Table 3.3 counts are compatible with all three (**ATLAS-EMP-001**; **ATLAS-EMP-002**).

### 5. What existing evidence supports or contradicts

**Supports treating sites as a material candidate constraint**

- ACCC: suitable sites limited; majors advantaged; planning/zoning can deter entry (**ATLAS-EMP-003**).  
- UK: more restrictive planning associated with fewer large-format stores — Griffith & Harmgart (**ATLAS-LIT-006**; https://doi.org/10.1080/09593969.2011.634071).  
- UK: Town Centre First associated with less productive store locations — Cheshire, Hilber & Kaplanis (2015) (**ATLAS-LIT-006** companion; https://doi.org/10.1093/jeg/lbu007).  
- UK CC: controlled land *can* barrier entry in highly concentrated local markets (**ATLAS-LIT-004**).

**Does *not* support a nationwide foreclosure premise**

- ACCC absence of land-banking determination (**ATLAS-EMP-002**).  
- UK CC absence of systematic sole-purpose holding — also an absence of finding, not contrary proof (**ATLAS-LIT-004**).  
- No AU public causal decomposition of M1a/M1b/M1c registered (**ATLAS-GAP-001**).

**Instrument-specific cautions (qualitative; not validated AU estimates)**

- **Sadun (2015):** restricting large formats can induce chain format shifts that harm independents (**ATLAS-LIT-003**).  
- **Conditional mechanisms for R vs E** (substitution between holding and exclusivity; incumbent re-bidding after release; reduced development incentives if exclusivity is banned in sparse markets): treated as **qualitative hypotheses**, with the development-inducement channel anchored to Schneier’s exclusive-dealing research — **not** as Phase 0D.2 equilibrium proof (**ATLAS-HYP-002**).  
- **UK CLO effectiveness** for consumer outcomes **not verified**; 2026 Aldi/Lidl designation is institutional only (**ATLAS-GAP-004**).

### 6. Plausible favourable, unfavourable and null pathways

| Pathway | R (develop-or-release) | E (exclusivity restriction) |
|---------|------------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Favourable** | Forces productive use or frees control where **M1c holding** was the binding block; rivals obtain released sites and open (**O1→O2**) | Removes contractual blocks while landlord still develops; rivals enter centres previously locked (**O1**) |
| **Unfavourable** | Destroys efficient **option value**; premature/low-productivity development; incumbent **self-develops** after release and still excludes rivals; planning (**M1b**) remains binding so release changes little | Landlord willingness to support development falls where exclusivity was an inducement; **Idle** capacity; substitution into ownership/option holding if E bans contracts but not fee-simple vacant control |
| **Null** | Sites were never commercially viable for rivals (scarcity/planning); release auctions won by incumbents; no change in fascia counts or prices | Exclusivity was not the binding constraint; CCA already constrained the worst clauses; centres redesign around other barriers |

### 7. Which O1–O6 outcomes can be assessed

| Outcome | Assessable now? | Basis |
|---------|-----------------|-------|
| **O1** | Partially (qualitative) | Pipeline facts; notification coverage of *new* major acquisitions; covenant text largely non-public |
| **O2** | Weakly | Release/develop rules speak to whether capacity appears; who operates is unidentified |
| **O3–O4** | Not from instrument text alone | Need demand and rivalry evidence |
| **O5–O6** | **Not** | No AU diversion/welfare estimates; forbid inference from site counts (**ATLAS-CON-003**) |

### 8. What cannot currently be inferred

- That unused Coles/Woolworths sites are predominantly strategic foreclosure.  
- That R or E would raise consumer welfare.  
- That R outperforms E (or the reverse) in Australian grocery — Phase 0D.2 explored related substitution in a stylised game and **rejected** it as an original contribution; those propositions are **not** treated here as established equilibrium results (**ATLAS-HYP-002**).  
- That UK controlled-land remedies “worked” in a welfare sense.  
- Constitutional/administrative feasibility of compulsory release or numeric caps.

### 9. Evidence that would materially change the answer

1. Parcel-level findings (ACCC/court) establishing exclusionary intent in a material share of long-held sites — or systematic rejection of that characterisation.  
2. Observed assignment of released sites to non-incumbents with subsequent openings.  
3. AU quasi-experiment decomposing planning delay vs holding vs exclusivity.  
4. Credible evaluation of exclusivity bans’ effects on *developed* grocery capacity (inducement channel).  
5. Specialist confirmation of a lawful, administrable release regime.

### 10. Carefully qualified conclusion

**On existing evidence, neither R nor E can be ranked as a welfare-improving reform.** Current law already scrutinises many major-supermarket *acquisitions* of qualifying land and applies foreign vacant-land development conditions where FIRB is triggered. The ACCC pipeline shows **many undeveloped sites** without proving **why** they are undeveloped. A serious reader can therefore evaluate site policies only by keeping **M1a / M1b / M1c / option value** as competing explanations, treating R and E as **additional** instruments with **sign-reversal risk** (premature development; re-acquisition after release; inducement failure under exclusivity bans), and refusing any claim that “releasing sites improves competition” without evidence on **who obtains the site** and **what consumers pay**.

## Case 2 — Foreign greenfield grocery investment

### 1. Precise policy question

For a **private** foreign grocery retailer seeking **greenfield** Australian capacity (new stores/DCs rather than buying an incumbent chain): **which FIRB/FATA requirements apply, and when could a truly binding requirement change entry timing or cost** — without inventing a uniform multi-year screening obstacle?

### 2. Legally accurate baseline

Transaction pathways must be branched (**ATLAS-CON-002**). Using the Phase 0B decision table (private non-FGI; Japanese-formed private acquirer eligible for certain FTA thresholds unless stated):

| Pathway | FIRB / FATA character (Layer A summary) | Atlas |
|---------|------------------------------------------|-------|
| **T1** Start Australian grocery business without land/business acquisition | **Not** generally notifiable under s 47; **is** reviewable NS action under s 55E(2) (call-in / voluntary notification) | ATLAS-LEG-001 |
| **T2** Acquire freehold **vacant** commercial land | Generally **$0** notifiable; typical productive-use / ~5-year construction **conditions** are a separate Guidance Note 4 practice point | ATLAS-LEG-003 |
| **T3** Acquire freehold **developed** non-sensitive commercial land | Certain FTA private: threshold **>$1,498m** (1 Jan 2026 summary) — many store-level sites may fall below | ATLAS-LEG-004 |
| **T5/T6** Long lease / AFL / option | May be land interest if >5-year limb (s 12) / agreement-option rules (s 15); then vacant vs developed thresholds apply | ATLAS-LEG-005 |
| **T8/T9** Acquire existing retailer (shares/assets) | Different pathways; **not** greenfield capacity addition | ATLAS-LIT-005 |
| **FGI contrast** | Start-business **must notify** (FATR s 56(1)(b)); land generally **$0** | ATLAS-LEG-002 |

**Administrative performance (Layer B):** For approved commercial proposals in Apr–Jun 2026, Treasury reports a **31-day median** and that **50%** were decided in 30 days or less — approval-cohort statistics, not guarantees (**ATLAS-ADM-001**; PDF: https://foreigninvestment.gov.au/sites/foreigninvestment.gov.au/files/2026-10/quarterly-report-apr-jun-2026.pdf). This rejects the MVP **60–240 month** baseline (**ATLAS-CON-001**).

**ACCC:** Part 3 supermarket rules apply to **major supermarket** acquirers (Coles/Woolworths connected entities), **not** automatically to a foreign entrant (**ATLAS-LEG-006**). General merger thresholds may still apply to large acquisitions.

**Planning:** Commonwealth FIRB/ACCC clearance **does not** substitute for State/local development consent.

**Institutional framing:** comparisons are against the post-2026 baseline, not a regulatory vacuum (**ATLAS-ASM-001**).

### 3. Counterfactual intervention

Define the intervention as one of the following **actual** relaxations (choose explicitly; do not blend) (**ATLAS-POL-003**):

| Counterfactual | What is relaxed | What is **not** relaxed |
|----------------|-----------------|-------------------------|
| **CF-A** | Lower scrutiny / faster process for **private** vacant-commercial **notifications**, or milder productive-use **conditions** | Planning clocks; site scarcity; DC investment |
| **CF-B** | Statutory or policy assurance reducing **s 55E call-in residual** anxiety for ordinary grocery start-business | Land notifications where thresholds bite |
| **CF-C** | Competition-purposive **greenfield preference** relative to acquisitions (Layer D; overlaps announced low-risk streamlining Layer C but is not identical) | National-security review; FGI rules |
| **CF-D** | Treating an **FGI** like a private investor for start-business notification | Broader national-interest/security tools |

**Rejected counterfactual:** “Turn off FIRB for all foreign grocery entry for 5–20 years.” That restates the corrected MVP myth (**ATLAS-CON-001**).

### 4. Affected causal mechanisms

- **M6a** transaction-specific FDI frictions (only if notifiable or called in).  
- **M6b** greenfield vs acquisition mode (ownership transfer ≠ new capacity).  
- **M1a/M1b** site and planning — often still bind after FDI clearance.  
- **M2a/M2b** scale and distribution — ACCC: capital unlikely binding for large internationals; sites/time-to-scale material (**ATLAS-EMP-003**).  
- **M5a-1** irreversible investment remains distinct from financing scarcity (**M5a-2** weakened for large internationals).

### 5. What existing evidence supports or contradicts

**Supports transaction-specific screening narrative**

- Phase 0B primary-text verification of ss 8B, 55E(2), FATR ss 17/56, land limbs (**ATLAS-LEG-001**–**005**).  
- Davies et al. (2018): greenfield and M&A FDI are not interchangeable (**ATLAS-LIT-005**).  
- ACCC: large-scale new chain entry unlikely short/medium term; ALDI >20 years (**ATLAS-EMP-003**).

**Contradicts uniform FIRB-as-main-barrier folklore**

- Private start-business not generally notifiable (**ATLAS-LEG-001**).  
- Approved-commercial median ~1 month in Q2 2026 (**ATLAS-ADM-001**).  
- MVP months-delay baseline unusable (**ATLAS-CON-001**).

**Does not establish**

- Approval *probabilities* by pathway.  
- That FDI liberalisation alone produces durable competition (**ATLAS-HYP-003** open).  
- Deal-specific critical-path effects of any single requirement.

### 6. Plausible favourable, unfavourable and null pathways

| Pathway | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Favourable** | CF-A speeds vacant-land approvals where FIRB notification/conditions were on the critical path; CF-C shifts marginal foreign capital from acquiring incumbents to building stores/DCs (**O2**); FGI-specific CF-D unlocks state-linked capital that was notification-blocked |
| **Unfavourable** | Faster vacant-land approval without planning reform produces land banking under weak conditions; greenfield preference gamed via nominal restructuring; acquisition of a failing independent is blocked while the independent exits anyway |
| **Null** | Binding constraints were sites, planning, or DC MES; FIRB was not on the critical path for the private entrant’s actual deal structure; call-in was never used for ordinary grocery starts |

### 7. Which O1–O6 outcomes can be assessed

| Outcome | Assessable? | Notes |
|---------|-------------|-------|
| **O1** | Qualitatively for notification incidence by pathway | Deal-structure dependent |
| **O2** | Only if greenfield capacity is the mode | Acquisition pathways may leave fascia counts unchanged |
| **O3–O6** | **Not** from FIRB relaxation alone | Format substitution and local rivalry unidentified in AU (**ATLAS-GAP-002**) |

### 8. What cannot currently be inferred

- A single “FIRB delay” parameter for foreign grocery entry.  
- That Japan-private and FGI entrants face the same screen.  
- That relaxing developed-land thresholds matters for typical store sites already below the FTA threshold.  
- Welfare gains from any CF-A–D.  
- That announced 1 Jan 2027 low-risk 30-day **performance targets** (Layer C) are already binding entitlements.

### 9. Evidence that would materially change the answer

1. Grocery-specific FIRB processing distributions by vacant land / lease / start-business / FGI.  
2. Documented call-in (or routine voluntary notification) of ordinary private grocery starts.  
3. Case studies where FIRB conditions were the marginal cause of abandonment after sites/planning were secured.  
4. Mode-specific AU evidence on greenfield vs acquisition competitive effects.  
5. Confirmation of Layer C streamlining commencement dates on the Federal Register.

### 10. Carefully qualified conclusion

**A serious FDI counterfactual must name the transaction class.** For a private greenfield grocer, incorporating and starting business is not the same legal object as buying vacant land, taking a long lease, or purchasing an incumbent. Existing evidence supports **mapping which requirements apply**, **separating notification triggers from typical conditions**, and **rejecting multi-year uniform FIRB delay** as the baseline story. It does **not** support claiming that “loosening FIRB” will deliver durable grocery competition unless site access, planning, and distribution scale are also feasible. Relaxation helps only where a **binding, correctly identified** requirement is removed — and even then **O5/O6 remain unidentified**.

## Case 3 — Differentiated entry and scale-up

### 1. Precise policy question

Using **ALDI’s Australian history** and **established entry literature**, what can be said — without new causal identification — about how a differentiated foreign grocer moves from entry to scale-up, and which outcomes (**O1–O6**) that history can and cannot discipline when evaluating pro-entry policy?

### 2. Legally accurate baseline

ALDI’s SA/WA expansion (announce 2013; openings 2016) occurred under **pre-2026** merger settings. The assessment uses that episode as a **historical mechanism test**, not as a description of today’s Determination Part 3.

Relevant **current** baseline for any *new* differentiated entrant:

- Private FDI branching as in Case 2.  
- Entrant is **not** a Determination s 1-7 major supermarket by default (**ATLAS-ASM-001**).  
- From 1 July 2026, very large retailers (covered revenue >$30b prior FY) are subject to the Code’s excessive-pricing **prohibition** under s 45B’s “in all the circumstances” standard (**ATLAS-LEG-009**) — a conduct rule, not a measured price effect.  
- ACCC inquiry characterisation of ALDI as important but limited-range constraint remains the AU institutional reading (**ATLAS-EMP-004**).

### 3. Counterfactual intervention

Not a single statute. The workable counterfactual class is:

> Policies that lower **site-access** or **FDI** frictions for a differentiated foreign format, holding constant the need to build **distribution capacity**, relative to a world that only liberalises FDI or only tightens merger notification.

Phase 0E already closed Strategy B as an **original-result programme**. This case uses the chronology as an **external-validity / falsification** object for that policy class.

### 4. Affected causal mechanisms

| Mechanism | ALDI-relevant content |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **M2a/M2b** | State DC projects completed/delivered in sequence with first openings — observed O2 order |
| **M1a/M1b/M1c** | Sites/planning/exclusivity appear in ALDI submissions nationally; **not identified** as the binding SA/WA timing cause |
| **M3b** | Limited-assortment hard discount — direct consumer benefits possible; incomplete incumbent price constraint |
| **M4a** | Incumbent price response **not** measured for SA/WA 2016–18 in PolisLab evidence |
| **M5a** | Multi-year irreversible investment; capital scarcity weakened for large internationals per ACCC |
| **M6b** | Foreign **greenfield** network addition (not acquisition of a major) — modes are not interchangeable (**ATLAS-LIT-005**) |

### 5. What existing evidence supports or contradicts

**Established chronology (O2 benchmark)**

| Milestone | Evidence |
|-----------|----------|
| Regency Park (SA) DC | BADGE project sheet: design-and-construct; **contract completion November 2015**; programme driven by early-2016 store rollout stocking (https://badge.net.au/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/ALDI-Distribution-Centre-1.pdf) |
| Jandakot (WA) DC | Georgiou project page: design-and-construct; duration **late 2014 – early 2016**; supports WA expansion (https://www.georgiou.com.au/project/aldi-distribution-centre/) |
| First SA stores | 3 Feb 2016 (contemporaneous news; Phase 0E) |
| First WA stores | 8 Jun 2016 (contemporaneous news; Phase 0E) |
| End-2018 aggregates | ALDI Economic Impact Report: **29** SA, **41** WA, **526** nationally (https://www.aldiunpacked.com.au/storage/2020/07/Economic-Impact-Report-May-2019.pdf) |

(**ATLAS-EMP-005**; **ATLAS-EMP-006**)

**ACCC descriptive anchors:** sites/planning material; capital unlikely binding for large internationals; ALDI >20 years nationally; limited-dimensional constraint (**ATLAS-EMP-003**; **ATLAS-EMP-004**).

**International format lessons (transfer mechanism only, not AU magnitudes)**

- Revenue diversion ≠ incumbent price cuts (**ATLAS-LIT-001**).  
- Warehouse-club/format entry can raise, null, or lower incumbent prices depending on format (**ATLAS-LIT-002**).  

**What Phase 0E could *not* discriminate:** H1 (distribution) vs H4 (ordinary sequencing); H2 (site access) not shown as the binding SA/WA timing constraint (**ATLAS-HYP-004**). Planning-approval storytelling is also cautioned by the Phase 0C.3A pilot (**ATLAS-PILOT-001**).

### 6. Plausible favourable, unfavourable and null pathways

| Pathway | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Favourable** | Differentiated entrant obtains sites, builds DC, opens stores, delivers **direct O6** via own prices/variety even if majors’ basket prices barely move; eventually constrains overlapping SKUs (**partial O5**) |
| **Unfavourable** | Site rules intended to help entrants trigger Sadun-style incumbent format shifts; entrant scale-up stalls without DC MES; majors’ local price response null while revenue diversion weakens independents |
| **Null** | FDI/site liberalisation does not change DC economics; announce-to-open still takes years; store growth without detectable incumbent price or welfare metrics |

### 7. Which O1–O6 outcomes can be assessed

| Outcome | From ALDI SA/WA + literature? |
|---------|-------------------------------|
| **O1** | Only narratively nationally; **not** identified for SA/WA timing |
| **O2** | **Yes — best-supported:** observed DC-project / opening order; post-opening densification to 2018 aggregates |
| **O3** | Weakly (queues; survival of format) — not structural demand |
| **O4** | Observed persistence through 2018 aggregates — not a causal survival model |
| **O5** | **No** causal AU price constraint estimate; ACCC limited-range characterisation only |
| **O6** | **No** — forbid inference from store counts (**ATLAS-CON-003**) |

### 8. What cannot currently be inferred

- That 2009–10 exclusivity undertakings *caused* SA/WA entry.  
- That Sunday trading caused WA entry.  
- Any O5/O6 number from 29/41 store counts.  
- That a specialty format would replicate ALDI’s diversion pattern.  
- US mile radii or Costco price elasticities as Australian forecasts.  
- That logistics **bound** SA/WA timing (as opposed to appearing in the observed order of investment).

### 9. Evidence that would materially change the answer

1. Store-level opening panel linked to local revenue **and** prices (AEMS-style) around ALDI (or other) openings.  
2. Documented announced-vs-opened catchment census with refusal/exclusivity reasons.  
3. AU diversion ratios between majors and hard-discount / specialty formats (**ATLAS-GAP-002**).  
4. Evidence of sustainable multi-store state presence *without* local DCs (would weaken H1).  

### 10. Carefully qualified conclusion

**ALDI’s SA/WA episode is a strong O2 scale-up benchmark and a weak O5/O6 guide.** Public contractor and company records show state distribution projects completed/delivered in sequence with first openings, followed by densification to 29 SA / 41 WA stores by end-2018. That is the **observed order of investment and openings**. Public evidence does **not** identify whether logistics, site assembly, or ordinary sequencing was the binding constraint, and does **not** isolate exclusivity or FDI liberalisation as the cause of timing. Literature warns that **format determines** whether incumbents cut prices. Policy propositions that equate “more ALDI-like stores” with “more consumer welfare” or “tighter incumbent price discipline” overclaim.

## Case 4 — Supermarket acquisition oversight

### 1. Precise policy question

What do the **ACCC notification regime**, **acquisition determinations**, and **notification waivers** establish about major-supermarket site control — and what are the limits of using merger enforcement as evidence for **market-wide** competitive outcomes?

### 2. Legally accurate baseline

| Instrument | Establishes | Does not establish |
|------------|-------------|--------------------|
| **Determination Part 3** (ss 1-7, 3-1–3-3) | Extra notification duties for Coles/Woolworths connected entities acquiring supermarket businesses or qualifying land/lease interests | That notified deals are blocked; that entrants face the same duties |
| **General Part 2 thresholds** | Broad mandatory suspensory notification when revenue/value tests met | Automatic SLC finding |
| **Notification determination** | Whether a **notified acquisition** may / must not be put into effect | Settlement, construction, or opening |
| **Notification waiver** | Whether notification is **required**; grant removes need to notify; **s 50 still applies**; refusal leaves any applicable notification and standstill obligations in place | Substantive “cleared as pro-competitive”; completed site control; prohibition of the deal |
| **Kalgoorlie Phase 2** | ACCC refusal of a **voluntarily notified** (27 Nov 2025) Coles leasehold; ACCC prospective SLC via independent asset-exit risk | Final Tribunal law; nationwide foreclosure doctrine; an example of *compulsory* Part 3 notification |

Sources: **ATLAS-LEG-005**; **ATLAS-LEG-006**; **ATLAS-LEG-007**; **ATLAS-LEG-008**; **ATLAS-ADM-002**; **ATLAS-ADM-003**. Baseline framing: **ATLAS-ASM-001**.

### 3. Counterfactual intervention

Compare:

- **Baseline B0:** post-1 Jan 2026 mandatory notification + Part 3 supermarket land/business rules + case-by-case SLC (**ATLAS-ASM-001**).  
- **CF-stronger:** Layer D presumptions against incumbent acquisition of challengers/sites, numeric caps, or automatic release (beyond SLC).  
- **CF-weaker:** sunset/non-renewal of Part 3 after 30 Jun 2030 without replacement; or treating waiver grants as sufficient competition assurance.

### 4. Affected causal mechanisms

- **M4b / M1c:** notification may deter or reshape preemptive site acquisitions (hypothesised; effects unidentified — **ATLAS-GAP-003**).  
- **M4a:** ordinary expansion via leasehold can be refused when the ACCC assesses diversion that threatens independent **asset** exit (Kalgoorlie) — **not** classified as land banking (**ATLAS-ADM-002**).  
- **M1a/M1b:** oversight of *acquisitions* does not create zoned sites or shorten planning clocks.  
- **O1 vs O2:** notification concerns control transactions; productive capacity requires later development.

### 5. What existing evidence supports or contradicts

**Institutional facts**

- Waiver page: grant removes notification need; s 50 continues; refusal leaves notification/standstill obligations if thresholds apply (**ATLAS-LEG-008**; https://www.accc.gov.au/business/mergers-and-acquisitions/notification-waivers).  
- **Kalgoorlie pathway:** Coles notified on **27 November 2025**, when notification under the new regime was permitted but **voluntary**; ACCC media release states it was therefore subject to the new regime’s requirements. Determination **30 June 2026** / public **1 July 2026**: acquisition must not be put into effect. The ACCC attributes a prospective independent-exit theory of harm (https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/accc-opposes-coles%E2%80%99-acquisition-of-a-supermarket-and-liquor-site-in-kalgoorlie-0; public reasons PDF on the acquisitions register).  
- **Tribunal status (official listing):** ACT 1 of 2026 listed for hearing **9–12 and 16–19 November 2026** (https://competitiontribunal.gov.au/current-matters/listings). Final merits orders/reasons not located for this release pass. **Limits**

- Kalgoorlie illustrates refusal power after voluntary notification; it should not be narrated as if it were a compulsory Part 3 filing example.  
- Diversion/welfare magnitudes remain unidentified (**ATLAS-GAP-002**).

### 6. Plausible favourable, unfavourable and null pathways

| Pathway | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Favourable** | Notification catches anticompetitive site/lease grabs; Kalgoorlie-type refusals preserve differentiated local constraints (**O5** locally); transparency enables scrutiny |
| **Unfavourable** | Parties shift earlier to non-notifiable structures; waiver pathway used for deals that still raise concerns (s 50 residual costly to enforce); refusal preserves a local independent but forgoes short-run capacity/consumer gains the ACCC itself acknowledged |
| **Null** | Most notified deals would have been cleared anyway; pipelines and openings unchanged; market-wide concentration driven by M2a sunk costs / M1b planning rather than acquirable sites |

### 8. What cannot currently be inferred

- Market-wide improvement in grocery competition from Part 3’s existence.  
- That waiver grants mean acquisitions are competitively harmless.  
- That notification approvals mean stores will open.  
- That Kalgoorlie settles Australian law on independent-exit theories while final Tribunal merits reasons are unpublished.  
- That merger enforcement substitutes for planning reform or greenfield FDI policy.

### 9. Evidence that would materially change the answer

1. Final Tribunal merits reasons in ACT 1 of 2026.  
2. Matured realisation study: notified/waiver sites → verified open / not open by date *T*.  
3. Causal design on Part 3’s effect on site preemption (hard on public data).  
4. Systematic covenant/exclusivity discovery beyond notification registers.  
5. Remake or repeal decisions around the 2030 sunset.

### 10. Carefully qualified conclusion

**Merger oversight is real, transaction-shaped, and outcome-incomplete.** From 1 January 2026, Coles/Woolworths qualifying land and supermarket-business deals face **compulsory** Part 3 notification (or waiver). Separately, Kalgoorlie shows that a **voluntarily notified** pre-commencement leasehold can still be **refused** under the new regime on an ACCC prospective SLC theory. Neither fact, nor the Jul–Sep 2026 pilot’s pathway mix, proves market-wide foreclosure reduction, measures consumer welfare, or shows that approved notifications become stores. A reader evaluating “stronger supermarket merger rules” should separate **(i)** process coverage, **(ii)** case-level ACCC theories, **(iii)** Tribunal finality, and **(iv)** unrealised development — and should not treat the public register as a competition scoreboard.

## What a reader can now evaluate more rigorously

1. **Whether a proposal attacks a real Layer A gap or restates existing tools** (Part 3 notification; foreign vacant-land conditions; Code excessive pricing).

4. **Why ALDI-like success stories do not license welfare claims** from fascia counts.
